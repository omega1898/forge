# Wire open checkers into FORGE's gates

FORGE can turn most of the open code-quality ecosystem into deterministic code, provided it treats licences, not languages, as the first design axis. **Full rule text may ship inside FORGE** for SEI CERT C, C++ and Java (now CC BY 4.0 text with MIT-licensed code examples), CWE 5.0 and CAPEC, the OpenSSF guides, the PEPs, Google's and Microsoft's style texts, the Safety-Critical Rust guidelines and fortran-lang's best practices. OWASP ASVS, the Top 10 and sqlstyle.guide can ship only as separate share-alike data files. **MISRA, AUTOSAR, HIC++, ISO/IEC 5055 and 24772 and the C++ Core Guidelines must be reduced to rule IDs, FORGE-written paraphrases and checks keyed to those IDs**, with users importing the licensed text themselves ([SEI LICENSE](https://github.com/cmu-sei/secure-coding-standards/blob/main/LICENSE); [MISRA tools](https://gitlab.com/MISRA/MISRA-C/MISRA-C-2012/tools)). Enforcement already exists in about thirty open checkers, from GCC, clang-tidy and cppcheck through GNAT, Fortitude, Clippy, staticcheck, ruff, SpotBugs, Roslyn, ESLint, ShellCheck, SQLFluff and checkov. Most emit SARIF 2.1.0; the rest need thin text or JSON adapters. **CWE 5.0, released 28 October 2026, is the join key**: its bundleable XML carries the 2025 Top 25, the CISQ/ISO 5055 measures, CERT and OWASP 2025 memberships, and per-weakness CERT rule mappings graded for fit ([CWE 5.0 XML](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/lmodel/cwe/main/upstream/cwec_v5.0.xml.zip)). Decidability is published for the large safety sets: 61 of 122 CERT C rules are marked detectable, 156 of MISRA C++:2023's 179 guidelines are decidable and 329 of AUTOSAR's 397 rules are automated, so the rest must become recorded human attestations ([CodeQL rules.csv](https://github.com/github/codeql-coding-standards/blob/main/rules.csv)). The payoff is large because the gap is large. Even after a test-feedback retry, most local-class models scored 5–16% on Aider's polyglot benchmark against 72–88% for frontier models. Larger models are not more secure. Analyzer-feedback loops cut GPT-4o's security findings from over 40% to 13% ([polyglot data](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/Aider-AI/aider/main/aider/website/_data/polyglot_leaderboard.yml); [Blyth et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2508.14419v1)). FORGE must not bundle Semgrep's registry rules, the CodeQL CLI or Sonar's analyzers, whose terms forbid exactly this use.

## Licences sort the corpus into three shippable tiers

For an offline assistant installed on other people's machines, the question that shapes the architecture is whether a standard's text may travel with the program. The licence files sort the corpus into three tiers. The first tier, free to embed with attribution, grew sharply in 2026. **SEI moved the CERT standards to GitHub under "a Creative Commons BY 4.0 Attribution License", with "code examples … licensed under a MIT license"** ([SEI LICENSE](https://github.com/cmu-sei/secure-coding-standards/blob/main/LICENSE)). The 2016 PDFs had required permission for "any other external and/or commercial use" ([SEI download page](https://cert.org/secure-coding/products-services/secure-coding-download.cfm)). The change matters for FORGE beyond compliance. Every CERT rule page carries noncompliant and compliant examples, a risk table, and Detectable and Repairable flags ([ERR33-C source](https://github.com/cmu-sei/secure-coding-standards/blob/main/content/4.sei-cert-c-coding-standard/03.rules/08.error-handling-err/4.err33-c.md)), which is exactly what a small model needs inside a repair prompt. The second tier is CC BY-SA content: OWASP's ASVS, Top 10 and Cheat Sheets, sqlstyle.guide and the Wikibooks Ada Style Guide. It can ship as separable data files, but any FORGE rewrite of it inherits share-alike ([ASVS README](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/OWASP/ASVS/master/README.md)). The third tier holds the safety-critical industry standards, whose text is paid or restricted. For these, FORGE may keep rule numbers, its own one-line paraphrases and its check logic, and nothing more.

The third tier already has a working precedent. **cppcheck's `misra.py` implements checks for 130 MISRA C:2012 rule numbers and ships no rule text**: "those should be downloaded from MISRA" and loaded with `--rule-texts` ([cppcheck manual](https://github.com/danmar/cppcheck/blob/main/man/manual.md)). Because MISRA's own headline file is non-commercial and no-derivatives, a distributed FORGE cannot bundle even that file. The user must import it, which FORGE's compliance layer already supports for control catalogs through `forge comply import-catalog` (`docs/design/compliance.md`). LLVM shows what happens otherwise: it deprecated `hicpp-*` in 22.x over licence issues and removed the module in 23.x ([LLVM 23 release notes](https://github.com/llvm/llvm-project/blob/release/23.x/clang-tools-extra/docs/ReleaseNotes.rst)). The C++ Core Guidelines look open but are not: the grant covers derivative works only "for your personal or internal business use" ([LICENSE](https://github.com/isocpp/CppCoreGuidelines/blob/master/LICENSE)). Three areas have no open coding standard at all: COBOL, assembly for any mainframe or embedded ISA, and JOVIAL. For these, FORGE's "standard" will be house rules written as checker configuration.

## About thirty open checkers already enforce these rules

Enforcement is not the bottleneck. Every language in scope except COBOL, VBA, JOVIAL and most assembly dialects has at least one maintained, OSI-licensed checker with stable rule IDs, and most of those checkers emit SARIF 2.1.0; COBOL has only compiler warnings and dialect settings. Copyleft tools such as GPL cppcheck, ShellCheck, hadolint, yamllint and pylint, and LGPL SpotBugs and Checkstyle, work as separately invoked sandbox processes, which is how FORGE already runs ShellCheck. Shipping their binaries in an offline installer still brings source-offer duties; this is general GPL practice, not a reviewed legal opinion. The tables below give, for each tool, its licence, its rule-ID scheme and standard mapping, its output format and its determinism.

### C and C++: CERT now lives behind clang-tidy aliases

The biggest recent change in C/C++ tooling is that **all 42 `cert-*` checks in clang-tidy (LLVM 23) are now aliases**, for example `cert-err33-c` for `bugprone-unused-return-value`, `cert-env33-c` for `bugprone-command-processor` and `cert-msc30-c` for `misc-predictable-rand` ([LLVM 23 list.rst](https://github.com/llvm/llvm-project/blob/release/23.x/clang-tools-extra/docs/clang-tidy/checks/list.rst)). A FORGE rule map must therefore key on the target check name and keep a version-aware alias table so that LLVM 21 and 22 installs still resolve. CERT's own Automated Detection tables are stale but useful as seed data. They credit Clang with 15 CERT C rules, open-source cppcheck with 20 and GCC with 12. Commercial Parasoft and Polyspace appear on 120 of 122 ([SEI repo](https://github.com/cmu-sei/secure-coding-standards/tree/main/content/4.sei-cert-c-coding-standard/03.rules)). That ratio shows how much of CERT coverage is commercial today. GitHub's MIT-licensed `rules.csv` is the best free crosswalk. It lists every MISRA C:2012/2023, MISRA C++:2023, AUTOSAR and CERT C/C++ rule with obligation, decidability and "Similar" cross-references ([rules.csv](https://github.com/github/codeql-coding-standards/blob/main/rules.csv)). The CodeQL CLI that runs those queries, however, may not be redistributed or used on non-open-source code without a paid GitHub Advanced Security licence ([CLI LICENSE](https://github.com/github/codeql-cli-binaries/blob/main/LICENSE.md)). FORGE should port query logic and reuse the crosswalk data, not ship the engine. The OpenSSF baseline is concrete enough to become a check on recorded build flags. It covers `-D_FORTIFY_SOURCE=3`, `-fstack-protector-strong`, `-fstack-clash-protection`, `-Wl,-z,relro -Wl,-z,now` and `-Werror=format-security`, and the guide ships a scraper that turns its options table into JSON ([hardening guide](https://github.com/ossf/wg-best-practices-os-developers/blob/main/docs/Compiler-Hardening-Guides/Compiler-Options-Hardening-Guide-for-C-and-C++.md)).

### Ada, Fortran, COBOL, Rust, Go, assembly and JOVIAL

For Ada, the compiler is the strongest checker FORGE can ship. **GNAT's `pragma Restrictions` and `pragma Profile (Ravenscar)` or `(Jorvik)` turn high-integrity subset advice into hard compile errors.** That advice includes the Ada 2022 RM H.4 restrictions such as `No_Allocators`, `No_Recursion` and `No_Exceptions` ([RM H.4](https://ada-rapporteur-group.github.io/ARM/Ada_2022/RM-H-4.html)). A local test during this research, on GNAT 13.3.0, confirmed that violations of `No_Allocators`, `No_Recursion` and `No_Exceptions` became compile errors, and that `-fdiagnostics-format=sarif-file` produced no SARIF for Ada. FORGE must therefore parse GNAT's `file:line:col` text, tagging each message with its switch via `-gnatw.d`. GNATcheck is the real Ada rule engine: GPL-3.0, LKQL rules, SARIF 2.1.0 output with embedded auto-fixes ([lkql_driver.rst](https://github.com/AdaCore/gnatcheck/blob/master/user_manual/source/lkql_driver.rst)). It has no Ubuntu or obvious community binary, so FORGE must build it against Libadalang. AdaControl depends on ASIS, which AdaCore no longer distributes to the community, and its last binary targets GNAT CE 2019 ([SourceForge](https://sourceforge.net/projects/adacontrol/)). For Fortran, **Fortitude (MIT) is the clear choice: 107 stable rules with ruff-style IDs and native SARIF** ([rules/mod.rs](https://github.com/PlasmaFAIR/fortitude/blob/main/crates/fortitude_linter/src/rules/mod.rs)). COBOL has no open linter with stable rule IDs. The deterministic substitute is GnuCOBOL's dialect system. Local inspection of GnuCOBOL 3.1.2's shipped dialect files showed per-feature support levels such as `alter-statement: unconformable` in `cobol2014.conf`, which a FORGE-authored `.conf` can raise to `error`; GnuCOBOL 3.2 has been out since July 2023 ([announcement](https://lists.gnu.org/archive/html/info-gnu/2023-07/msg00007.html)). Rust and Go have the richest permissive stacks. Assembly and JOVIAL have almost nothing.

## Roughly half to nine-tenths of each rule set is machine-decidable

Decidability decides where a rule sits in FORGE: as a blocking gate, as a warning backed by whole-program evidence, or as a human attestation. The safety standards publish this classification. MISRA classes each rule as decidable, meaning "a static analysis tool can check in every possible case", or undecidable, and separates single-translation-unit rules from system-scope rules, whose compliance "can only be established as a property of an entire system" ([Polyspace](https://www.mathworks.com/help/bugfinder/ug/polyspace_coverage_coding_standard.html); [Qt](https://www.qt.io/software-insights/misra-c2023-guidelines-the-intricacies-of-undecidability)). CERT's equivalent is the per-rule Detectable flag: "Can a static analysis tool automatically determine if code violates this guideline with high accuracy and precision?" It sits beside a Repairable flag: "Can an automated repair tool reliably fix an alert by making local changes?" ([SEI](https://github.com/cmu-sei/secure-coding-standards/blob/main/content/4.sei-cert-c-coding-standard/02.front-matter/2.introduction/03.how-this-coding-standard-is-organized.md)). CERT's flags are expert judgements, not formal decidability proofs.

|---|---|---|---|

| MISRA C++:2023, 179 guidelines | **156 decidable** | 20 undecidable; 4 directives not supportable; 3 unclassified | [rules.csv](https://github.com/github/codeql-coding-standards/blob/main/rules.csv) |
| AUTOSAR C++14, 397 rules | **329 automated**, 22 partially | 46 non-automated | [rules.csv](https://github.com/github/codeql-coding-standards/blob/main/rules.csv) |

The numbers support a three-way triage that FORGE can encode as data. MISRA decidable single-TU rules and CERT rules marked Detectable become **blocking findings**. Undecidable and system-scope rules become **warnings that need evidence from a whole-program or sound analyzer** (IKOS, Frama-C EVA or GNATprove), where a "proved safe" verdict is stronger than any absence of findings. Directives, CERT rules marked not detectable and ASVS requirements that no tool covers become **attestation items** under FORGE's existing `forge comply attest`. Deviations are recorded in the style of MISRA Compliance:2020, which MISRA publishes as a free download ([MISRA forum](https://forum.misra.org.uk/showthread.php?tid=1529)). CERT's risk table adds two more machine-readable signals. Its Severity, Likelihood and Remediation Cost multiply into a priority and level (ERR33-C, for example, is P27, level L1), which maps directly onto FORGE's severity field. Its Repairable flag says which alerts may enter an automatic fix loop at all. Determinism needs one caveat. Path-sensitive engines (the Clang Static Analyzer, GCC `-fanalyzer`, Infer Pulse, cppcheck value-flow) give the same output for fixed inputs, but their findings shift with exploration budgets, timeouts and versions. FORGE must record the budgets with the tool digest, as it already records compiler digests.

## CWE 5.0 and SARIF give FORGE one finding model

The cross-language layer is where FORGE gains the most per line of code. **CWE 5.0, dated 28 October 2026, introduces XML Schema 8.0 with breaking changes**: categories are now "Groupings" and the namespace is `cwe-8`. The zip FORGE should pin has SHA-256 `bcda44c9…73a3dc`. It must be validated against `cwe_schema_v8.0.xsd` rather than the "latest" link, which pointed to an earlier 8.0 draft that the catalogue breaks in 69 places ([lmodel about](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/lmodel/cwe/main/docs/about.md)). The XML is a ready-made compliance engine. Membership tests on view 1435 answer "is this in the 2025 Top 25?"; that list was built from 39,080 CVE records ([CISA](https://content.govdelivery.com/accounts/USDHSCISA/bulletins/3ff772a)). Views 1306–1309 answer the CISQ Reliability, Maintainability, Security and Efficiency questions. Views 1154 and 1133 cover CERT C and CERT Java. View 1450 covers OWASP Top Ten 2025 and view 1449 the OpenSSF Python guide. The per-weakness `Taxonomy_Mappings` provide 227 CERT C mappings across 118 weaknesses, each graded: CWE-476 maps to EXP34-C as "Exact", while CWE-190 maps to INT30-C only as "CWE More Abstract" ([CWE 5.0 XML](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/lmodel/cwe/main/upstream/cwec_v5.0.xml.zip)). FORGE should present only "Exact" fits as equivalences. It should also use each entry's mapping-usage field (755 Allowed, 100 Allowed-with-Review, 85 Prohibited, 44 Discouraged) to push vague tool CWEs down the view 1000 hierarchy before reporting.

Two cautions keep these claims honest. First, CISQ counts 74 Reliability, 73 Security, 18 Efficiency and 29 Maintainability weaknesses, while the CWE 1305 views list 34, 34, 15 and 28 members ([CISQ via NIST](https://www.nist.gov/document/responses-enhancing-software-supply-chain-security-cisq)). FORGE can claim "ISO 5055-aligned tagging", never ISO 5055 conformance. Second, CWE 5.0 still titles view 1450 "RC1 (2025)" although OWASP has released the final Top 10:2025, whose intro gives slightly different per-category CWE counts ([Top10 README](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/OWASP/Top10/master/README.md)). FORGE should source OWASP memberships from OWASP's own CC BY-SA category pages when the two disagree.

SARIF supplies the interchange. GitHub reads rule metadata from `shortDescription`, `fullDescription`, `properties.tags`, `security-severity` (above 9.0 critical, 7.0–8.9 high) and `precision` ([GitHub SARIF docs](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/github/docs/main/content/code-security/reference/code-scanning/sarif-files/sarif-support.md)). CodeQL encodes CWE as `external/cwe/cwe-NNN` tags ([style guide](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/github/codeql/main/docs/query-metadata-style-guide.md)), and SARIF's own `taxonomies`, `taxa` and `relationships` objects carry it first-class ([SARIF tutorial](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/microsoft/sarif-tutorials/main/docs/3-Beyond-basics.md)). **Cross-run identity can be reproduced offline.** GitHub uses only `primaryLocationLineHash`, a rolling hash over the first 100 non-whitespace characters of each line, with MOD 37 and an occurrence counter ([fingerprints.ts](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/github/codeql-action/main/src/fingerprints.ts)). FORGE's current `fingerprint()` already hashes the rule ID, path and whitespace-normalized line text; computing GitHub's hash as well would let FORGE baselines round-trip with GitHub code scanning. The merge recipe that follows from these sources is short. FORGE resolves every artifact URI to repository-relative form and prefixes each rule ID with its tool name. It extracts CWE IDs from relationships, tags, `CWE-N` strings and `properties.cwe`, and validates them against the pinned XML. It then computes its own fingerprint and deduplicates across tools on CWE set, file, line hash and a small line window, keeping every contributing (tool, rule) pair as provenance. Finally it emits one run sorted by file, line, column and rule, with UUIDv5 GUIDs, so that identical inputs give byte-identical output.

For languages with no adequate checker (VBA, JOVIAL, COBOL embedded SQL, house assembly rules), FORGE needs its own rule engine. ast-grep is MIT, tree-sitter and its core grammars are MIT, and the ast-grep-essentials rules are Apache-2.0, so all can ship ([ast-grep](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/ast-grep/ast-grep/main/LICENSE); [tree-sitter](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/tree-sitter/tree-sitter/master/LICENSE); [essentials](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/coderabbitai/ast-grep-essentials/main/LICENSE)). ast-grep has no SARIF printer, so FORGE would emit SARIF itself. The Semgrep and Opengrep engines are LGPL-2.1 and usable with FORGE-authored rules ([Opengrep README](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/opengrep/opengrep/main/README.md)). The registry rules around those engines are another matter.

## Deterministic loops, not bigger models, narrow the gap

The case for building all of this rests on how small models fail. On Aider's polyglot benchmark (225 hard Exercism tasks in six languages; data last updated October 2025, so it misses 2026 local models), frontier models reach 72–88% once they see unit-test errors. Most local-class models stay in single digits or the teens ([polyglot data](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/Aider-AI/aider/main/aider/website/_data/polyglot_leaderboard.yml)).

| Model | First try | After one test-feedback retry |
|---|---|---|
| gpt-5 (high) | 52.0% | 88.0% |
| claude-opus-4 (32k thinking) | 37.3% | 72.0% |
| DeepSeek-V3.2-Exp Reasoner | 39.6% | 74.2% |
| Qwen3 32B | 14.2% | 40.0% |
| Qwen2.5-Coder-32B-Instruct (whole-file format) | 4.9% | 16.4% |
| Codestral 25.01 | 4.0% | 11.1% |
| gemma-3-27b-it | 1.8% | 4.9% |

Two findings in that data point directly at FORGE features. First, **format reliability is a large share of the small-model deficit**. Qwen2.5-Coder-32B produced well-formed diffs only 71.6% of the time and QwQ-32B 67.6%. Pairing QwQ as reasoner with Qwen Coder as a separate editor raised well-formed output to 100% and the score from 20.9% to 26.2%. Second, test feedback multiplies small-model scores by about 2–3.5x but adds only 3–26 points, against 35–41 points for frontier models, so feedback narrows the relative gap without closing it. SWE-bench tells a similar story at the agent level. Moatless reports claude-3-5-haiku at 28% against claude-3-5-sonnet at 46% on the Verified Mini subset ([Moatless README](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/aorwall/moatless-tools/main/README.md)).

Security is different, because scale does not help. **Veracode's tests of more than 100 LLMs found insecure choices 45% of the time and that "larger models did not perform significantly better than smaller ones"**, with Java failing over 70% of the time ([Veracode](https://www.veracode.com/press-release/ai-generated-code-poses-major-security-risks-in-nearly-half-of-all-development-tasks-veracode-research-reveals/)). CyberSecEval found that "models with superior coding abilities were more susceptible to suggesting insecure code" ([arXiv 2312.04724](https://arxiv.org/html/2312.04724v1)). CWEval measured about a 30% gap between functional and functional-and-secure pass rates ([CWEval](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2501.08200)). A SonarQube study of five models on 4,442 Java tasks found that 90–93% of issues in the generated code were code smells. It also found that moving from Claude 3.7 Sonnet to Sonnet 4 nearly doubled the share of BLOCKER bugs, from 7.1% to 13.71% ([arXiv 2508.14727](https://arxiv.org/abs/2508.14727v1)). Correctness, security and maintainability are therefore three independent gates. A frontier escalation needs the same security gate as a local patch, which FORGE's hybrid layer already enforces by passing frontier patches through the same evaluator.

The remedies with evidence are all deterministic. **Iterative prompting with Bandit and Pylint output cut GPT-4o's security issues from over 40% to 13%, readability violations from over 80% to 11% and reliability warnings from over 50% to 11% within ten iterations** ([Blyth et al., SCAM 2025](https://arxiv.org/abs/2508.14419v1)). CodeQL-and-Bandit-driven repair, with GPT-3.5 or GPT-4 as generator and repairer, reached 65.9–85.5% success. The same study found that models repaired their own code poorly, a self-repair "blind spot" ([Gong et al.](https://arxiv.org/html/2408.10495v2)). Olausson et al. found self-repair gains "often modest" once cost is counted, and limited by the model's ability to critique itself. Feedback from a stronger model helped substantially ([arXiv 2306.09896](https://arxiv.org/abs/2306.09896)). For a small local model, analyzer output therefore beats self-critique, and escalating the critique (not the whole task) to a frontier model is a cheaper middle path. Harness design adds more. SWE-agent's edit tool runs flake8 before and after every edit and reverts any edit that adds errors ([edit tool](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/SWE-agent/SWE-agent/main/tools/windowed_edit_linting/bin/edit)). Removing its custom edit action reportedly cut SWE-bench Lite from 18.0% to 10.3%, a secondary figure ([review](https://www.themoonlight.io/review/swe-agent-agent-computer-interfaces-enable-automated-software-engineering)). Agentless samples several patches and lets regression and reproduction tests choose among them, reaching 27.3% on Lite at about $0.34 per issue ([Agentless](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/OpenAutoCoder/Agentless/main/README.md)). Constrained decoding is the only family with direct evidence of a small model beating a much larger one. With monitor-guided decoding, the 1.1B SantaCoder beat text-davinci-003 on compilation rate ([MGD, NeurIPS 2023](https://neurips.cc/virtual/2023/poster/70362)). Type-constrained decoding cut TypeScript compile errors by more than half ([PLDI 2025](https://pldi25.sigplan.org/details/pldi-2025-papers/25/Type-Aware-Constraining-for-LLM-Code-Generation)), and SynCode's authors report removing 96.07% of syntax errors ([SynCode](https://par.nsf.gov/servlets/purl/10633733)). The main unknown remains. **No study found holds the harness fixed and measures analyzer-feedback loops on 7–32B local models**, so FORGE must measure this itself.

## Twelve FORGE features turn the research into code

FORGE already has most of the scaffolding. The D02 analyzer normalizes ruff output into SARIF-shaped `NormalizedFinding` records with a versioned `RULE_TABLE` (revision 1) and a line-text fingerprint, and it computes the review verdict from findings, "never a model's opinion" (`forge/assessment/analyzers.py`). `forge check analyze` already runs GNAT `-gnatwa`, gfortran `-Wall -Wextra`, GCC `-fanalyzer`, `cobc -Wall` and ruff (`docs/design/check.md`). The compliance layer imports licensed catalogs and OSV data and records attestations, and the model gateway already constrains model slots to grammar-safe schemas (`README.md`). The features below extend those hooks rather than adding prose skill files.

The build order follows the evidence. Features 1–3 come first, because every later feature consumes normalized, CWE-tagged, decidability-classed findings, and they immediately upgrade the six toolchains `forge check` already runs. Features 6 and 7 come next. They are the best-evidenced levers for small models: lint-and-revert targets the 28–32% malformed-edit rate, and analyzer-driven repair targets security, where model scale does not help. Feature 10 must ship before FORGE claims that the gap is closed. D11 already demands that the whole confidence interval on an improvement lie above zero, and Juliet's public-domain licence lets the checker corpus travel with FORGE. Juliet is synthetic and has known defects, so its scores must sit beside a realistic set such as OWASP Benchmark or CWEval. Feature 9 depends on whether the local inference runtime exposes logit masks, which this research did not verify.

## Conclusion

The research changes the picture of what "baking standards into FORGE" means. The valuable asset is not a library of prose skills, which the pack licences mostly allow but which enforce nothing. It is a **rule registry that records, per rule, what text may ship, which pinned tool check enforces it, which CWE it maps to and whether a machine can decide it**. Every input to that registry is now openly licensed: CodeQL's crosswalk, CERT's Detectable and Repairable flags, CWE 5.0's views and graded mappings, and the tools' own CWE tables. The 2026 CERT relicensing is the inflection point. It lets FORGE hand a small local model the actual compliant example for the rule it just broke, something MISRA's text can never supply in a distributed tool. The licence trend runs the other way for rule registries and migration linters: Semgrep, Sonar, Atlas and Liquibase all closed their rules or linters in 2024–25. That makes self-authored rules on open engines (MIT ast-grep and tree-sitter, LGPL Opengrep) a durable choice, not a stopgap.

The evidence also changes what FORGE should claim. Deterministic security gates matter for every model, local or frontier, because security does not scale with model size. That makes the gates part of the product's core value, not a crutch for weak models. Whether analyzer-feedback loops lift 7–32B models as much as they lifted GPT-4o is unmeasured anywhere. FORGE's sealed-split D11 harness, fed with Juliet, CWEval and BaxBench, could produce the first fixed-harness answer. That number, not any frontier leaderboard, should decide how far FORGE trusts its local loop before offering escalation.
